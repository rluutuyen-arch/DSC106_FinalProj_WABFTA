What to Look For

SST as Storm Fuel

Tropical cyclones are heat engines: they extract energy from warm ocean water and release it as wind and rain. Scientists generally set 26°C as the threshold needed to sustain a tropical storm. In this visualization the Caribbean and Gulf of Mexico stay well above that mark throughout the 72-hour window — the bright orange and yellow pixels in the map represent water between 28–33°C, exactly the thermal reservoir that Isaias was drawing from as it organized and intensified westward across the Lesser Antilles.

Watch the storm marker as you scrub through the frames. On July 28 it is still a disorganized tropical wave in the far eastern Atlantic, moving across relatively cooler open-ocean water (~27°C). By July 30 it has crossed into the warmest part of the Caribbean basin and rapidly strengthened from a tropical depression into a hurricane in less than 36 hours — a process meteorologists call rapid intensification. The mean SST chart on the right shows a corresponding uptick in basin-wide temperatures over that same period.

Reading the Map

The color scale runs from deep purple (cool, ~16°C) through orange to yellow (warm, ~32°C). The solid track line traces Isaias's path up to the current frame; the faint dashed line shows where it will go. Hover over the storm eye marker for wind speed and position. You can also zoom and pan — try zooming into the Caribbean to see how closely the storm tracks along the 29–31°C band. The two red warning triangles on the mean SST chart flag frames with incomplete satellite coverage (sensor noise or cloud interference). Toggle "Show 26°C fuel zone" to dim every pixel below the tropical-cyclone threshold, leaving only the warm water that can actually sustain a storm — then scrub to July 30 and watch the eye sit deep inside the lit-up reservoir.

Challenges & Design Decisions

Converting GOES-16 satellite data to screen coordinates required implementing the full GOES-R fixed-grid projection, which maps satellite scan angles to geographic positions. Small errors in that transform produce visually distorted maps, so we validated the output against known coastline positions. Rendering 72 hourly frames of thousands of SST pixels at interactive speeds required binning raw data points into a 50×35 grid before drawing, which reduces draw calls by roughly 40× while preserving the spatial structure of the SST field.

The storm track is derived from NHC HURDAT2 best-track data and interpolated to each hourly frame. Because HURDAT2 records positions every 6 hours, the animated eye position is linearly interpolated between advisory fixes — an approximation that is accurate enough to illustrate the SST–storm relationship but should not be treated as a precise storm-center location for any given hour. The "Storm Fuel" card and the dashed sampling ring around the eye report the mean SST of the pixels immediately surrounding the storm, alongside the share of the basin above 26°C, tying the thermal field directly to the storm's available energy.
